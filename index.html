Frontier energy research

Zero-carbon energy from new frontier physics.

We are building and testing a gold cavity whose geometry is designed to break a symmetry that has so far kept the vacuum’s electromagnetic field out of reach as an energy source.

The physics

The vacuum is not empty. Getting energy from it is the hard part.

Decades of experiment show that the electromagnetic zero-point field is real and measurable. A theorem by Timothy Boyer explains why simple cavities cannot turn it into work, and also what would have to change.

Measured

The Casimir force

Two uncharged conducting plates, very close together, attract each other because the gap excludes electromagnetic modes that are too long to fit. Predicted by Hendrik Casimir in 1948; first precisely measured by Steve Lamoreaux in 1997.

Measured

The Lamb shift

Willis Lamb measured a small shift in hydrogen’s energy levels in 1947 that arises from the electron’s interaction with vacuum fluctuations. It earned the 1955 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Demonstrated

The dynamical Casimir effect

In 2011, Christopher Wilson and colleagues at Chalmers University of Technology showed that rapidly changing a cavity’s boundary conditions produces real, detectable photons.

Wilson et al., Nature, 2011.

The obstacle

Boyer’s conservative-force theorem (1975) shows that a static Casimir cavity with mirror-symmetric boundaries is conservative. Whatever the cavity takes from one part of the field spectrum it returns to another, so no net work can be drawn over a cycle.

Boyer’s work on stochastic electrodynamics spans more than five decades, from his 1968 derivation of the Casimir force.

Our route

The theorem depends on symmetry. If the boundary treats left-handed and right-handed modes differently, the detailed balance it relies on no longer has to hold. Rather than move the walls, we change their shape: a chiral, self-similar interior inside a tetrahedral enclosure.

This is our hypothesis. It has not been confirmed by measurement.

The device

The zero-point field spectral modulator

One enclosure, two counter-wound spirals, one axis. Each element has a job.

  1. A tetrahedral gold enclosureFour faces, six edges, four vertices. A regular tetrahedron has no centre of inversion, which adds a second break in mirror symmetry, and its A₄ rotation group restricts which modes the cavity can support. It also acts as a Faraday cage.
  2. Two counter-wound spiralsOne wound left-handed, one right-handed, interleaved without touching. Together they present different boundary conditions to left- and right-circular modes.
  3. A golden-ratio pitchThe radius grows by the golden ratio φ every quarter-turn (b = 2ln(φ)/π ≈ 0.3063), so the geometry repeats at every scale and modifies modes across several decades of frequency, not one.
  4. Three turns, on a C₃ axisEach spiral turns exactly three times about the axis through one vertex and the centre of the opposite face: twelve quarter-turns, matching the order of the tetrahedron’s rotation group.

Why gold

A clean boundary

High conductivity (about 4.1 × 10⁷ S/m at 293 K) approaches the ideal conductor of Casimir theory. It electropolishes below the roughness at which Casimir corrections grow, does not oxidise, and is diamagnetic, so the electromagnetic contribution can be isolated.

Why this geometry

Chosen, not tuned

Three independent arguments give the same three-turn answer: scale coverage, the order-12 tetrahedral rotation group, and the winding number of a C₃ axis. We treat the specification as geometrically determined, and will test whether that holds.

Arrays

Scaling by repetition

Multiple cavities can be coupled to a common load, with spiral axes aligned so that their effects add. Whether they do is a question for the beta prototype stage.

How we will know

Three independent measurements

A claim that the cavity modifies the zero-point field has to survive more than one kind of instrument. We plan to use three, each able to fail on its own.

Measurement 1

Casimir force mapping

An atomic force microscope or torsion pendulum, introduced through an access port, measures the force between a probe and the interior spiral surface as a function of position and orientation.

Measurement 2

Spectral emission

A calibrated photodetector or bolometer compares the cavity’s spectral radiance, from microwave to near-infrared, with a thermal reference at the same temperature. A departure from Planck’s law that geometry cannot explain would be evidence.

Measurement 3

Interferometry

A Mach–Zehnder or Fabry–Pérot interferometer compares a beam through the cavity with a reference beam through free space, looking for a change in effective refractive index from altered mode density.

Applications

Value along the way

The same geometry has uses that do not depend on net energy extraction. Energy transduction is the most ambitious, and the least proven.

Near term

Broadband shielding

Mode suppression across many decades of frequency, for sensitive electronics and precision measurement.

Near term

Chiral mode selection

Preferential transmission of one circular polarisation, relevant to circular dichroism spectroscopy and optical communications.

Medium term

Casimir micro-actuation

Stronger, more tuneable multi-scale Casimir forces for MEMS and NEMS actuation and precise positioning.

Hypothesis

Energy transduction

A passive geometric rectifier for ambient field fluctuations, first for low-power sensors, then scalable clean energy. Subject to further device development.

Roadmap

A five-year research programme

Each year ends with a decision. If the measurements do not support the next step, we stop or change direction and report why.

  1. Year 1

    Theory and baseline

    Assemble the interdisciplinary team, build the theoretical framework and thermodynamic analysis, establish ultra-high-vacuum capability and baseline Casimir measurements.

  2. Year 2

    Materials and fabrication

    Develop fabrication of the chiral cavity structures, establish detection systems and electromagnetic field measurement, and automate test sequences.

  3. Year 3

    Subsystem testing

    Refine cavity geometry from Year 2 findings, build energy-output sensor arrays and calorimetry, begin long-duration stability testing.

  4. Year 4

    Alpha prototype

    Integrate all subsystems into a first complete prototype in a sealed, fully monitored environment, and test across operating conditions.

  5. Year 5

    Beta and assessment

    Build an optimised beta prototype with parallel cavity arrays, compare theoretical and measured performance, and assess scalability honestly.

Integrity

What we have not shown

Extraordinary claims need ordinary rigour. These are the questions a sceptical physicist should ask us, and our commitments in answering them.

Open risks

Where this could fail

  • The core hypothesis may fail: net extraction may prove impossible or impractical.
  • Thermodynamic constraints may prohibit net energy extraction.
  • Any effect may work at micro-scale and not scale up.
  • Fabrication tolerances may exceed what is currently achievable.
  • Distinguishing true output from experimental artefacts is hard.

Commitments

How we will work

  • Report all findings, negative results included.
  • Seek peer review of key theoretical and experimental outcomes.
  • Verify any energy claim independently, using multiple measurement methods and rigorous controls.
  • Keep a clear line between proven results and speculative potential.
  • Convene a quarterly independent review board.

Team

Disciplines we need

Theoretical physicists, quantum technologists, materials scientists, and vacuum, measurement and control engineers, led by a principal investigator and a chief technology officer.

Governance

Independent oversight

An ethics advisory board led by a philosopher of science, and an energy strategist to plan transition and equitable access.

Responsibility

Licensing and access

Tiered licensing by sector, open elements for basic research, and preferential terms for humanitarian applications.
